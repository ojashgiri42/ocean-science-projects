0. THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS
========================================================================

In the Atlantic, warm salty water flows north near the surface, cools,
sinks, and returns south at depth. This "overturning circulation"
carries a lot of heat. Two things set the density of sea water:
temperature (cold water is heavy) and salt (salty water is heavy). At
high latitudes the cold makes the water heavy, which drives the
overturning. But rain, rivers and melting ice make the water fresher,
and fresh water is light, which works against it.

The overturning also feeds itself: it carries salty water north, which
keeps the northern water heavy. If it weakens, less salt arrives, the
water gets lighter, and it weakens more. This is the "salt-advection
feedback". Because of it, the same climate can allow two different
circulations: a strong one and a weak (or reversed) one. Switching
between them is a "tipping point". The Atlantic overturning is on the
list of possible tipping elements of the climate system (Lenton et al.
2008).

In 1961 Henry Stommel showed this with two boxes of water joined by a
pipe. In 1994 Paola Cessi simplified it to one equation and added
random "weather" noise. I used both models to ask:
  1. Are there really two stable states, and how do they lose
     stability?
  2. If the forcing changes slowly, does the state jump, and does it
     come back when the forcing is undone (hysteresis)?
  3. Can noise alone flip the circulation, and how long does it wait?
  4. Does the system give a warning before it tips?
  5. Could something similar happen at the edge of an Antarctic ice
     shelf? (a toy model of my own)

Spoiler: yes to the first four, with honest error bars, and "it depends
on things nobody has measured well" for the fifth.

These are dimensionless conceptual models. They teach the mechanism;
they do not predict the real ocean.

4. STEP 1: STOMMEL'S TWO BOXES AND THEIR STEADY STATES
========================================================================

Notebook: 01_stommel_equilibria.ipynb

The model. T and S are the temperature and salinity differences
between the boxes (dimensionless). Both are pulled back toward 1 by the
surroundings ("restoring"): temperature quickly, salinity slowly
(delta = 1/6). The flow f through the pipe is driven by the density
difference: f = (-T + R S)/lambda. f < 0 means a temperature-driven
flow (like today's overturning), f > 0 a salinity-driven one, running
the other way. The flow mixes the boxes at a rate |f|.

Why two states are possible at all: salinity is restored more weakly
than temperature. Stommel's condition is R delta < 1; here it is 1/3.
If salinity were held as tightly as temperature, the salt-advection
feedback would have nothing to work with.

What I did:
  4.1 At steady state, T = 1/(1+|f|) and S = delta/(delta+|f|). Put
      these into the flow law and you get one equation in f alone. I
      plotted both sides (Stommel's graphical method) and found where
      they cross with brentq.
  4.2 For each equilibrium I computed the 2x2 Jacobian (how the
      tendencies change when T or S is nudged) and its eigenvalues.
  4.3 I ran forward Euler from 10 starting states.

Results:
  equilibrium        f (mine)  f (Stommel)  eigenvalues        type
  temperature-driven -1.0679   -1.1         -3.610, -0.761     stable node
  middle             -0.3070   -0.30        -2.849, +0.761     saddle
  salinity-driven    +0.2191   +0.23        -0.912 +/- 1.823i  stable spiral

  8 of my 10 starting states ended in the temperature-driven state.
  These are hand-picked points, not a measure of how big each basin is.

Time step check: the end states with dt and dt/2 agree to 1.34e-13,
but that proves nothing (see mistake 1 in section 12). The whole paths
agree to 1.91e-3 with dt = 0.002.


========================================================================
5. STEP 2: PUSHING STOMMEL'S MODEL OVER THE EDGE
========================================================================

Notebook: 02_stommel_hysteresis.ipynb

The idea: make the pipe slowly harder to flow through (raise lambda)
and watch the temperature-driven state.

What I did:
  5.1 Found all equilibria at 701 values of lambda between 0.1 and
      0.45.
  5.2 Found the fold (where the temperature-driven state and the
      saddle meet and vanish) in two independent ways: bisection, and
      the maximum of lambda(f) = rhs(f)/f.
  5.3 Swept lambda from 0.1 up to 0.45 and back down in steps of
      0.005, running 200 time units at each step and starting each step
      from where the last one ended. That memory is what makes
      hysteresis possible.

Results:
  - Fold at lambda = 0.333800 (bisection) and 0.333801 (maximum), at
    f = -0.4837. lambda(f) at f = -0.5 is exactly 1/3, a good quick
    estimate.
  - Stommel's estimate from his graph, "slightly greater than 1/4", is
    too low: at lambda = 0.26 and 0.30 the temperature-driven state
    still exists (2 equilibria with f < 0).
  - On the way up, f was -0.5331 at lambda = 0.330 and jumped to
    +0.2042 at lambda = 0.335.
  - On the way down, f stayed positive the whole way (smallest
    +0.1937) and ended at +0.2328 at lambda = 0.1, where the sweep had
    started at f = -1.8542. Undoing the change did not bring the
    temperature-driven state back, as Stommel had already said.
  - Every step settled (largest leftover tendency 9.97e-12).

Just past the fold the system moves very slowly before it jumps. That
slowness is the seed of the early warnings in step 5.

6. STEP 3: CESSI'S ONE-EQUATION MODEL AND ITS LANDSCAPE
========================================================================

Notebook: 03_cessi_landscape.ipynb

The model:
    dy/dt = p - y (1 + mu2 (1 - y)^2),     mu2 = 6.2

  y   salinity difference. Small y = strong overturning.
  p   freshwater forcing (evaporation minus rain), which builds up y.
  y * 1              slow diffusion between the boxes.
  y * mu2 (1 - y)^2  salt carried by the flow. The flow is proportional
                     to the density difference, 1 - y, and Cessi made
                     the exchange grow with the SQUARE of the flow
                     (Stommel used |f|). This keeps the equation smooth.
  Temperature is fixed at 1, because it relaxes in about 25 days,
  far faster than salinity.
  Time is in "diffusion times", about 219 years each.

Because there is one variable, dy/dt = -dV/dy for a potential V(y):
picture a ball rolling downhill in a landscape, through thick syrup.
Valleys are stable states, hills unstable ones.

Results:
  - Folds at p = 1.2962 (y = 0.4272, where the strong state ends) and
    p = 0.9556 (y = 0.9061, where the weak state ends). Between them
    there are three equilibria. Cessi's Fig. 4 puts the first fold at
    about 1.3. I found no published value for the second.
  - At p = 1.1: strong valley y = 0.2402, hill 0.6911, weak valley
    1.0687, the same as Yang et al. (2021). The barrier from the strong
    valley to the hill is 0.05710, from the weak valley 0.03560.
  - Ramping p from 0.8 to 1.5 and back over 500 diffusion times each
    way (109,500 years), the jumps came 0.0182 past each fold. With a
    ramp 4 times slower, 0.0072. The ratio, 2.53, is close to
    4^(2/3) = 2.52, the scaling expected for a slow ramp through a fold.
    Only two ramp speeds, so this is a consistency check, not a proof.

7. STEP 4: ADDING WEATHER NOISE (KRAMERS' LAW)
========================================================================

Notebook: 04_cessi_noise.ipynb

The model: the same equation with a random kick added every time step
(white noise of size sigma), integrated with the Euler-Maruyama method.
The kick scales with sqrt(dt), not dt, because random kicks add up like
a random walk.

What I did:
  7.1 One long run at p = 1.1, sigma = 0.2: 20,000 diffusion times
      (4.38 million years). A flip counts only when y goes all the way
      from one side to the other (two thresholds, at 0.4656 and
      0.8799), so wiggles near the hilltop are not counted.
  7.2 Compared the histogram of y with the exact long-run distribution,
      proportional to exp(-2V/sigma^2).
  7.3 Kramers' law: at 6 noise levels from 0.18 to 0.30, measured the
      mean time spent in the strong state. Kramers (1940) predicts that
      ln(time) against 1/sigma^2 is a straight line with slope
      2 x barrier = 0.1142.
  7.4 Computed the EXACT mean time to cross (the mean first-passage
      time), which needs no weak-noise approximation.
  7.5 Time step check with 20,000 walkers started at the low threshold.

Results:
  - Example run: 411 flips. Mean stay in the strong state 71.5
    diffusion times (15.7 kyr), in the weak state 25.4 (5.6 kyr).
  - y was below the hill 73.9 +/- 2.4% of the time; theory says 72.4%.
  - Fitted slope 0.1188 +/- 0.0026 (95% interval 0.1117 to 0.1260),
    which includes Kramers' 0.1142.
  - The waiting times are 4-17% shorter than Kramers' formula. Two
    reasons: my timer starts at the low threshold, partway up the
    barrier (the exact time from the valley bottom is 5-16% longer),
    and the noise is not weak (2 x barrier / sigma^2 is only 1.3 to
    3.5).
  - The exact passage times agree with the simulations within 1.1
    standard errors at the four weaker noise levels. At sigma = 0.276
    and 0.30 the simulations are 14% and 8% longer.
  - Time step check at sigma = 0.30: 11.518 +/- 0.086 with dt = 0.01,
    11.144 +/- 0.083 with dt = 0.0025, exact 11.057. So dt = 0.01 adds
    about 4% here, and the error shrinks with dt.

8. STEP 5: CAN WE SEE THE TIP COMING? (EARLY WARNING SIGNALS)
========================================================================

Notebook: 05_early_warning.ipynb

The idea ("critical slowing down"): near a fold the valley becomes
flat, so the pull back to the strong state, the recovery rate r, goes
to zero. A system that recovers slowly lets each kick last longer, so
its wobbles get bigger (variance rises) and each value looks more like
the one before (lag-1 autocorrelation rises). Linear theory says
variance = sigma^2/(2r) and lag-1 autocorrelation = exp(-r dt).

What I did:
  8.1 Ramped p from 1.0 to 1.4 over 4000 diffusion times with weak
      noise (sigma = 0.04).
  8.2 Kept one value every 0.5 diffusion times (like a proxy record),
      removed the slow trend with a centred 50-unit rolling mean, and
      stopped 10 units before the tip (otherwise the jump itself leaks
      into the statistics).
  8.3 Computed variance and lag-1 autocorrelation in trailing windows
      250 units long, and their trend with Kendall's tau (+1 = always
      rising, 0 = no trend).
  8.4 Repeated everything for 100 runs with different random numbers,
      and for 100 control runs at a fixed p = 1.1 (no fold).

Results:
  - The recovery rate falls from r = 2.947 at p = 1.00 (74 years) to
    0.349 at p = 1.29 (627 years), and reaches 0 at the fold.
  - The run shown tipped at p = 1.2695 (t = 2695.3 diffusion times,
    590 kyr), before the fold at 1.2962: near the fold the barrier is
    so low that noise pushes y over the hill early.
  - Kendall tau 0.880 (variance) and 0.735 (autocorrelation). Variance
    in the last window 9.54e-4, against 8.56e-4 from linear theory.
  - 100 ramp runs: Kendall tau 0.775 to 0.912 (variance) and 0.664 to
    0.855 (autocorrelation), 5-95% range. 100 control runs: -0.301 to
    0.337 and -0.322 to 0.350. The two groups never overlap.

Cautions: this is retrospective (it uses the known tip time, as in
Dakos et al. 2008). It used one noise level and one ramp speed. And if
noise alone causes a jump far from any fold, there need not be any
warning (Ditlevsen and Johnsen 2010).

9. STEP 6: A TOY MODEL OF AN ANTARCTIC ICE-SHELF MARGIN
========================================================================

Notebook: 06_antarctic_toy_model.ipynb

This model is my own toy, not taken from a paper; every parameter is an
order-of-magnitude guess. Warm/cold switches of ice-shelf cavities are
known from regional ocean models (Hellmer et al. 2012; Hazel and
Stewart 2020), but there the switch depends on which water mass fills
the cavity. My toy tests a different feedback:

  warmer water at depth -> more melting -> fresher surface water ->
  weaker vertical mixing -> less heat lost upward -> warmer water at
  depth

    dT/dt = (T_far - T)/tau_in - kappa(F) T - b m T^2
    dF/dt = c m T^2 - F/tau_F

  T      subsurface temperature above freezing (deg C)
  F      surface freshening (psu)
  T_far  far-field ocean temperature (the control knob)
  kappa  vertical mixing rate, weakened by freshening, in two forms:
         kappa0/(1 + F/F0)  ("rational") or kappa0 exp(-F/F0)
  m T^2  basal melt, quadratic in temperature (m = 10 m/yr per deg C^2)
  c      0.0154 psu of freshening per m of ice melted
  b      0.0187 deg C of cooling per m of ice melted

Results, with kappa0 = 5 per year and F0 = 0.2 psu:
  - Rational law: no threshold. A threshold would need kappa0 of at
    least 13.66 per year.
  - Exponential law: a threshold. The state jumps to the warm branch at
    T_far = 3.505 deg C going up, and back at 3.149 going down.
  - Why they differ, worked out by hand: without the melt-cooling term,
    a fold needs kappa0 tau_in > 8 (rational) or > 2.24 (exponential),
    whatever the other parameters are. The notebook finds 8.000 and
    2.241. The difference is how fast the heat loss kappa T can fall as
    the water warms.
  - A fairer comparison (exponential law with F0 = 0.289 psu, so both
    laws halve the mixing at 0.2 psu) still gives a threshold, at
    3.923 and 4.258 deg C.
  - At T_far = 2 deg C the shelf water is only about 0.36 deg C above
    freezing: a threshold needs strong mixing, and strong mixing keeps
    the shelf cold. In this model, thresholds belong to cold shelves.

So the toy model cannot say whether a real ice-shelf margin has a
threshold. It does show what would decide it.

10. THE BIG PICTURE
========================================================================

  1. The salt-advection feedback gives two stable states for the same
     forcing, as long as salinity is held less tightly than
     temperature.
  2. Slow forcing can push the system past a fold; it jumps, and
     undoing the forcing does not bring it back (hysteresis).
  3. Noise alone can flip it. The waiting time grows exponentially with
     barrier / sigma^2, as Kramers' law says; when the noise is not
     weak, the exact first-passage formula is the better benchmark.
  4. Approaching a fold, the system slows down, and variance and
     autocorrelation rise. In 100 runs out of 100 this was clear.
  5. These ideas carry over to ice shelves, but whether a real
     threshold exists depends on processes that are poorly known.

What I would do next: run the same hysteresis experiment in a real
ocean model (project 15 does a related freshwater-hosing experiment in
MITgcm), and couple the ocean to
an ice sheet, so that meltwater depends on the ocean state.

12. MISTAKES I MADE AND HOW I CAUGHT THEM
========================================================================

  1. A test that could not fail. My first time step check compared end
     states, which agree for any dt (an Euler fixed point is the true
     equilibrium). I added a comparison of whole paths.
  2. Too little settling time. With 100 time units per lambda step, one
     step just past the fold still had a leftover tendency of 2.5e-2.
     The system lingers where the state used to be. Fixed with 200.
  3. The tip inside the warning. My first early-warning statistics shot
     up at the very end, because the jump itself got into the windows.
     Fixed by stopping 10 diffusion times before the tip.
  4. Too short a sweep step in the toy model (30 years): one point was
     caught halfway past a fold. Fixed with 100 years.
  5. A "check against the paper" whose source I had not checked: my
     first table of Cessi's values listed "expected" numbers without a
     source. I traced them: the first fold to Cessi's Fig. 4, the
     valleys and hill to Yang et al. (2021). For the second fold I
     found none, and now I say so.
  6. A wrong description: I wrote that Cessi's flow grows with
     (1 - y)^2. The flow grows with 1 - y; it is the exchange that goes
     with its square.
  7. A wrong explanation: I said Kramers' formula sits above my points
     only because the noise is not weak. Computing the exact
     first-passage time showed a bigger reason: my timer starts partway
     up the barrier. A careful time step test then showed dt = 0.01
     adds about 4%.
  8. A missing density: I treated 1 m of melted ice as 1 m of fresh
     water. The freshening was 12% too strong; correcting it moved the
     toy model's thresholds from 2.943/3.300 to 3.149/3.505 deg C.
  9. Theory at the wrong point: I first compared each window with
     linear theory at the window's last point, which overestimates the
     variance near the fold. Now I average the theory over the window.
 10. One seed. I first had a single noisy run. 100 runs and 100
     controls now show the result is not luck.
 11. Overclaiming: I wrote that my warning test "only uses the past".
     The detrending is centred and the cut-off uses the known tip
     time, so the test is retrospective.
 12. Missing credit: Stommel had already estimated the fold and noted
     the hysteresis in 1961, and the early-warning idea comes from
     Held and Kleinen (2004) and Dakos et al. (2008). Now cited.
 13. A notebook saved after running cells out of order. Restart and
     run all, every time.

13. WORDS TO KNOW (GLOSSARY)
========================================================================

  overturning        the large loop of sinking and rising water that
  circulation        carries heat northward in the Atlantic
  box model          an ocean described by a few well-mixed boxes
  salt-advection     the flow brings salt, salt keeps the flow going
  feedback
  restoring          a boundary condition that pulls a value back to a
                     target (as opposed to a fixed flux)
  equilibrium        a state where nothing changes
  stable / unstable  a small push dies away / grows
  node, spiral,      kinds of equilibrium: approached directly;
  saddle             approached while circling; approached along one
                     direction but pushed away along another
  Jacobian           table of how each tendency changes when each
                     variable is nudged; its eigenvalues give stability
  fold (saddle-node) where a stable and an unstable state meet and
                     disappear
  hysteresis         the state depends on history, not only on the
                     current forcing
  potential V(y)     the landscape whose slope drives the system
  barrier            height of the hill between a valley and the next
  forward Euler      new value = old value + time step x tendency
  Euler-Maruyama     the same, plus a random kick of size
                     sigma x sqrt(time step)
  white noise        random kicks with no memory
  Kramers' law       the mean escape time grows like
                     exp(2 x barrier / sigma^2)
  mean first-passage the exact average time to reach a point for the
  time               first time
  critical slowing   near a fold the system recovers from kicks very
  down               slowly
  recovery rate r    how fast a small kick decays
  lag-1              how much each value resembles the one before
  autocorrelation
  Kendall's tau      a number for a trend: +1 always up, 0 no trend
  detrending         removing the slow drift before measuring wobbles
  retrospective      analysed afterwards, knowing when the tip happened
  batch means        uncertainty from the spread of averages over equal
                     parts of a run
  diffusion time     Cessi's time unit, about 219 years
  thermal forcing    water temperature above the local freezing point
  ice shelf          the floating part of an ice sheet
  basal melt         melting at the underside of an ice shelf
