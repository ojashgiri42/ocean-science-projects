========================================================================
0. THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS
========================================================================

Around Antarctica, the ice sheet flows off the land into the sea and
floats. The floating part is an "ice shelf": a slab of ice hundreds of
metres thick, with ocean water underneath it in a big hidden space called
the "cavity". The place where the ice first lifts off the seafloor is the
"grounding line".

Ice shelves act like a cork in a bottle: they hold back the ice on land
behind them. If they get thinner, the land ice flows faster into the sea
and sea level rises. Most ice-shelf thinning today comes from the ocean
melting them FROM BELOW, not from warm air above.

The question of this project: how fast does the ocean melt an ice shelf,
and how does that depend on
  - how warm the ocean is (the main question),
  - the uncertain numbers inside the melt formula,
  - and the model's grid size?

A key number: "thermal forcing" = how many degrees the water is above its
freezing point. Earlier studies (Holland, Jenkins and Holland 2008) found
that melt grows FASTER than in simple proportion to thermal forcing:
double the thermal forcing and the melt more than doubles. In my project
14 toy model I simply assumed melt = constant x (thermal forcing)^2. This
project tests that with a real 3D ocean model.

Spoiler: the melt grew roughly like thermal forcing to the power 1.5 in
my setup, but WHERE the warm water goes matters as much as how warm it is.

    bash scripts/build.sh ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/source_copy/code ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/build_reference_fast fast

  official answer. "fast" = optimized, for the long runs.
  Both built first time.

  Later I needed other builds, because some changes are only possible at

  my changed files, given as the 4th argument of build.sh:
    build_safe_fast     + experiments/safety_code   (NaN-safe check, 3.4)
    build_rbcs_fast     + experiments/rbcs_code     (adds the restoring
                                                     package, section 7)
    build_fine_fast     + experiments/fine_code     (a grid twice as fine,

    build_isoplus_fast  + experiments/isomip_plus_code (ISOMIP+ grid and
                                                     melt formula, sec. 12)

Step 3.4 - A safety change I carry over from project 15. In project 15 one
  run "blew up" (its numbers became NaN, "not a number") but still printed
  "ended normally", because the model's built-in check did not notice NaN.
  So every build from step 2 on includes a corrected copy of the check
  (mon_solution.F; the test is written so that NaN fails it), the monitor
  runs every 30 model days (monitorFreq = 2592000 seconds), and
  run_experiment.sh refuses to call a run good if "NaN" appears anywhere
  in the model's own output lines.

4. CHECKING THE BUILD AGAINST THE OFFICIAL ANSWER (STEP 1)
========================================================================

Why: before changing anything, I must know my build gives the right
answer. Otherwise I could never tell a real result from a computer
problem.

How: run the experiment's short test exactly as provided (20 time steps
of 30 minutes = 10 model hours):
    bash scripts/run_experiment.sh ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/build_reference_ieee \
        ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/source_copy/input ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/reference_test_ieee
and compare its printed "%MON" (monitor) lines with results/output.txt,
counting how many digits agree (notebooks/01_build_and_verify.ipynb).

A trap: the model starts perfectly still with perfectly uniform water, so
in the first steps many monitored numbers are pure rounding noise (for
example a temperature spread of 8e-14 in a field that should be exactly
uniform). Comparing noise is meaningless, so I compared monitor times
10-20, after the water had started moving, and left out the mean sea
level (which is held at zero; its values are noise of about 1e-21).

Result (50 quantities):
  - IEEE build: worst agreement 9.6 digits, typical 13.4 digits.
  - fast build: worst 7.5 digits, typical 10.8 digits.
Agreement to 7.5 or more digits means my build solves the same equations
the same way.

Timing: one model month took 39.3 s with the fast build, so about 8
minutes per model year. The IEEE build was 10.5 times slower. So all long
runs used the fast build.


========================================================================
5. UNDERSTANDING THE SETUP: AN ICE SHELF INSIDE AN OCEAN MODEL
========================================================================

5.1 The box
  - A piece of ocean from 80 S to 70 S and 15 degrees of longitude wide,
    on a grid of 50 x 100 points (0.3 degrees east-west, 0.1 degrees
    north-south; cells about 6-11 km x 11 km).
  - 30 layers, each 30 m thick; flat seafloor at 900 m.
  - The ice shelf covers EVERYTHING. Its base is 700 m deep at the
    southern wall (the grounding line side), rises to 200 m by 76 S, and
    stays at 200 m to the north.
  - Walls on the first row and first column. Because the grid wraps
    around, these walls also close the other sides: a sealed box.
  - The water starts still, at -1.9 C and salinity 34.4 g/kg everywhere.
  - No wind, no surface, no rivers. The ONLY thing that can move the water
    is melting and freezing at the ice base.

5.2 How an ocean model holds an ice shelf
  MITgcm normally has fixed horizontal layers starting at the sea surface.
  The ice-shelf package (pkg/shelfice) pushes a "lid" down into them:
  - Cells above the ice base are "dry" (they are ice).
  - The first wet cell under the ice acts like the sea surface: the heat,
    salt and fresh water from melting go in there.
  - The ice base usually falls between two layers, so that top cell is
    only partly open ("partial cell"). The smooth ice base becomes a
    staircase.
  - The ice's weight presses on the water. A file gives this extra
    pressure ("load anomaly"), so the floating ice is in balance.
  - IMPORTANT: the ice never changes shape. Melt is computed as a flux,
    but the ice does not get thinner in the model.

5.3 The melt formula: the "three equations"
  At the ice base, three things must be true at once:
    1. Heat: heat arriving from the ocean = heat used to melt ice
       + heat conducted up into the cold ice.
    2. Salt: salt arriving from the ocean = salt needed to make the fresh
       meltwater as salty as the water at the ice base.
    3. Freezing point: the water touching the ice is exactly at its
       freezing temperature, which depends on salinity and pressure:
         T_freeze = -0.0575 x S + 0.0901 - 0.000761 x pressure(dbar)
  Unknowns: melt rate, temperature at the ice base, salinity at the ice
  base. The model solves this every time step at every point.
  How fast heat and salt cross the thin water layer touching the ice is
  set by two "transfer coefficients":
         gamma_T = 0.0001 m/s for heat (default),
         gamma_S = 0.00505 x gamma_T for salt (salt moves ~200 times
         more slowly than heat in water).
  The test as provided uses a simpler ISOMIP formula. From step 2 on I
  switched to the three-equation model (in data.shelfice:
  useISOMIPTD = .FALSE.), because it is what modern models use.

5.4 Why cold water can melt ice deep down
  Pressure lowers the freezing point: about 0.75 C colder per 1000 m.
  At salinity 34.4: -1.89 C at the surface, -2.04 C at 200 m,
  -2.42 C at 694 m. So the -1.9 C water is BELOW freezing at the surface
  but 0.5 C ABOVE freezing at 700 m. It can melt the deep ice.

5.5 Units I used
  The model reports melt as a fresh-water flux, SHIfwFlx, in kg per m^2
  per second, positive UPWARD, so melting is a negative number.
      melt (m of ice per year) = -SHIfwFlx / 917 x 31 536 000
  917 kg/m^3 = ice density; 31 536 000 s = a 365-day year (this setup has
  no calendar). Example: -0.00001 kg/m^2/s = 0.344 m of ice per year.
  (ISOMIP+ in step 7 uses metres of WATER per year and a 365.2422-day
  year, because its rule book says so.)

6. THE BASELINE RUN AND THE "ICE PUMP" (STEP 2)
========================================================================

What I changed (experiments/baseline/):
  - data.shelfice: useISOMIPTD = .FALSE.   (three-equation melt)
  - data:          nTimeSteps = 87600      (5 years of 30-minute steps)
                   globalFiles = .TRUE.    (one output file per field)
                   pChkptFreq = 31536000.  (restart file every year)
                   monitorFreq = 2592000.  (check every 30 days)
  - data.pkg:      useDiagnostics = .TRUE. (time-averaged output)
  - data.diagnostics: 30-day means of the melt flux (SHIfwFlx), heat flux,
                   transfer coefficient and friction velocity; yearly
                   means of 3D temperature, salinity and velocity.

First a 60-day test (experiments/baseline_test/) to check the output
files and the sign of the melt. Then the 5-year run (43 minutes, 108 MB):
    bash scripts/run_experiment.sh ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/build_safe_fast \
        ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/source_copy/input ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/baseline experiments/baseline/input

What I found (notebooks/02_baseline_cavity.ipynb):
  - The mean melt fell fast, then slowly: 0.92 m/yr in the first month,
    0.084 after 1 year, 0.037 after 4.9 years. It was still falling 10.5%
    per year at the end. A sealed box has no heat supply: every bit of
    melting uses up the water's heat, so the melt can never settle.
  - Melting at the deep end (0.87 m/yr where the ice base is 681 m deep),
    REFREEZING higher up (-0.16 m/yr at the 200 m ice next to the slope).
  - A layer of cold, fresh meltwater along the ice base, down to -2.43 C
    and 34.19 g/kg.
  - One overturning loop of 160 mSv (thousandths of a Sverdrup; 1 Sv =
    1 million m^3 per second): water goes south at depth and north up
    along the ice base.

The ice pump (the main idea of step 2):
  1. Deep water is above its local freezing point (pressure!), so it
     melts the deep ice.
  2. The meltwater is fresh, so the mixture is light. It rises along the
     sloping ice base as a plume.
  3. As it rises, the pressure drops and its freezing point goes UP. The
     water, cooled at depth, is now colder than its new freezing point:
     "supercooled".
  4. It refreezes onto the ice higher up ("marine ice").
  Ice is moved from deep to shallow, driven only by how pressure changes
  the freezing point.

Lesson: to study warm water, I must keep adding heat somewhere.


========================================================================
7. KEEPING THE OCEAN WARM, ATTEMPT 1: A MISTAKE WORTH LEARNING FROM
========================================================================

The tool: MITgcm's "rbcs" package (relaxation boundary conditions). In
cells where a "mask" is above zero it nudges temperature and salinity
toward a target:
    change per second = -mask x (T - T_target) / tau
tau is the nudging time. Adding the package means a new build
(experiments/rbcs_code/packages.conf has one extra line, "rbcs").

A trap: rbcs uses mask number 1 for temperature and mask number 2 for
salinity. If you only give mask 1, salinity is silently NOT nudged (the
model just prints a warning). So data.rbcs sets both:
    relaxMaskFile(1)='rbcs_mask.bin',
    relaxMaskFile(2)='rbcs_mask.bin',

Attempt 1 (experiments/rbcs_*): leave the ice everywhere and nudge the
northernmost 1 degree (north of 71 S, under the flat 200 m ice) over 10
days, toward -1.9, -1.4, -0.9 or +0.1 C, for 5 years.

Why it failed (notebook 03, Part B1):
  - The nudged water sat right against the ice, so it melted the ice
    above it: 0.55 to 9.5 m/yr there, against 0.05 to 1.2 m/yr in the
    rest of the cavity. My forcing was making most of the melt.
  - The warmth never reached the deep cavity: in the coldest case, 1189
    columns south of 74.55 S melted exactly as in the sealed box.
  - The zone did not even reach its target (about -0.07 C instead of
    +0.1 C), because the melting above it kept cooling it.
  A power-law fit gave an exponent of 1.92, but it was measuring my
  forcing, not the ocean. I kept these runs only as a lesson.


========================================================================
8. KEEPING THE OCEAN WARM, ATTEMPT 2: AN OPEN-OCEAN ZONE (STEP 3)
========================================================================

The fix (the same idea ISOMIP+ uses): nudge in OPEN ocean, with no ice
above it.

  1. Remove the ice north of 71 S (notebook 03, Part A): ice draft = 0 and
     ice weight = 0 there. I first checked that the weight file depends
     only on the ice draft (cells with the same draft have exactly the
     same weight), so 0 weight is right where the ice is gone. New files:
     experiments/open_geometry/icetopo_open_north.bin and
     phi0surf_open_north.bin. The ice shelf now ends in a 200 m ice
     cliff ("ice front") at 71 S, with open water north of it.
  2. The nudging mask is 0 at the ice front and rises to 1 at the
     northern wall, at all depths, only in the open water.
  3. tau = 1 day (strong), so the open water stays at its target.
  4. Four cases with targets -1.9, -1.4, -0.9 and +0.1 C (the baseline
     plus 0.5, 1 and 2 C). Salinity target 34.4 everywhere.
  5. Each runs 10 model years from the cold start. In data.rbcs:
         tauRelaxT=86400.,  tauRelaxS=86400.,
         relaxMaskFile(1)='rbcs_mask_open.bin',
         relaxMaskFile(2)='rbcs_mask_open.bin',
         relaxTFile='theta_rbcs_p10.bin',   (for the -0.9 C case)
         relaxSFile='salt_34p4.bin',
  6. All four at once, one core each (2 hours 54 minutes):
         bash scripts/run_open_cases.sh ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/inputs_rbcs base p05 p10 p20
  A 30-day test first: zero melt in the open water, sea level within
  4.5 cm, largest CFL number 0.08 (CFL = how far water moves in one step
  compared with a grid cell; it must stay well below 1).

Thermal forcing: far-field temperature minus the freezing point at the
deepest ice (694 m, -2.421 C): 0.521, 1.021, 1.521 and 2.521 C.

What I found:
  - "Steady" means the melt changes by less than 2% from one year to the
    next. After 10 years the two warm cases were steady; the two cold
    ones were still slowly falling. So I continued those two for 10 more
    years from their restart ("pickup") files:
      experiments/open_base_ext and open_p05_ext; the only change in
      their "data" file is   nIter0 = 175200,   ("start at step 175200",
      the end of year 10). Run with:
      bash scripts/run_extension.sh ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/inputs_rbcs base_ext
    The -1.4 C case settled in its second decade. The -1.9 C case was
    STILL falling after 20 years (by 3.7% per year), so its number is an
    upper limit.
  - Melt (m of ice per year): 0.025, 0.104, 0.207 and 0.415 for far
    fields of -1.9, -1.4, -0.9 and +0.1 C.
  - Best fit through the three steady cases: melt = 0.104 x TF^1.52.
    With all four: TF^1.79. Both are between 1 (simple proportion) and 2
    (my project 14 assumption).
  - Why above 1: meltwater is light, so more melt drives a stronger flow
    out of the cavity, and warm water flows in to replace it. The inflow
    across the ice front was 11, 79, 116 and 268 mSv, growing roughly
    like TF^2. More melt pulls in more heat.
  - The surprise: about 30% of the melt comes from the single row of ice
    right behind the ice front (up to 8.8 m/yr), while the DEEP ice (near
    the grounding line) barely responds to the warming (0.11 to 0.14
    m/yr in every case). The deep cavity stayed at about -2.0 C even
    when the open ocean was +0.1 C.
  - Why: my warm water has the same salinity as the cavity water, so
    warming it makes it LIGHTER. At 700 m depth the +2 C water was 0.08
    kg/m^3 lighter than the deep cavity water, so it could not sink to
    the grounding line; it flowed in under the flat ice instead. (The
    -1.9 and -1.4 C water was still slightly denser, and there the old
    ice-pump circulation survived.) In the real Antarctic the warm water
    (Circumpolar Deep Water) is also saltier and therefore heavier, so it
    DOES reach the grounding line. This is a limitation of my simple
    setup, and ISOMIP+ (step 7) uses a warm, salty deep layer instead.


========================================================================
9. HOW MUCH DO THE MELT SETTINGS MATTER? (STEP 4)
========================================================================

The question: the melt formula has two "transfer coefficients" (gamma_T
for heat, gamma_S for salt) that nobody knows well. If I get them wrong
by a factor of 2, how wrong is my melt rate?

The reference: the -0.9 C case from step 3 (open_p10), because it had
settled. (I first started these runs from the -1.9 C case, then stopped
them after 8 minutes when I saw that case had not settled, and restarted.
A comparison is only fair against a reference that is not still
changing.)

Three new runs, each a copy of open_p10 with ONE line changed in
data.shelfice:
  open_g05:   SHELFICEheatTransCoeff = 0.5E-4,   (gamma_T halved)
  open_g20:   SHELFICEheatTransCoeff = 2.0E-4,   (gamma_T doubled)
              (gamma_S follows automatically: it is 0.00505 x gamma_T)
  open_frict: SHELFICEuseGammaFrict = .TRUE.,    (coefficients depend on
              the current speed next to the ice, Holland and Jenkins 1999)
              SHELFICEDragQuadratic = 2.5E-3,    (turning the option on
              would quietly change the friction of the ice base too; this
              line keeps it the same as in the other runs, so only ONE
              thing changes)
  Run all three at once (2 h 56 min):
      bash scripts/run_open_cases.sh ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/inputs_rbcs g05 g20 frict

What I found (mean of year 10, m of ice per year):
  gamma_T halved      0.180   (0.87 x the reference)
  gamma_T default     0.207
  gamma_T doubled     0.234   (1.13 x)
  speed-dependent     0.059   (0.28 x)

  - A factor of 4 in gamma_T changed the melt by only about 30%. Why? A
    bigger gamma_T takes heat out of the water touching the ice faster,
    so that water gets colder and closer to freezing, which slows the
    melting again. The real limit is how fast the circulation brings
    warm water INTO the cavity, not how fast heat crosses the thin layer.
  - Making the coefficients depend on the current speed made a much
    bigger difference: 3.5 times less melt. The currents next to the ice
    in this cavity are very slow (about 1 mm/s), so the coefficient
    became about 100 times smaller than the constant default
    (about 0.000001 m/s instead of 0.0001).
  - A detail I found in the code: the model never lets the speed count as
    less than 1 mm/s. A quarter of the ice sat exactly at that minimum
    all year. So that result partly depends on a choice made by the code
    writers, not on physics.


========================================================================
10. CHECKING THE BOOKKEEPING AND SUMMING UP (STEP 5)
========================================================================

10.1 The salt check. Melting adds fresh water, so the ocean's salt
  content must drop by an amount I can predict from the melt the model
  reports. If it does not, something is wrong in the model or in my
  analysis.
  - I used the sealed baseline (step 2): nothing else can change its
    salt.
  - In this setup the meltwater is not added as extra volume; instead the
    model removes salt ("virtual salt flux"). The salt removed per second
    = (fresh-water flux) x (salinity right at the ice base) / 1030.
  - Result: after 1800 days the mean salinity had dropped by 0.018765
    g/kg; my prediction from the melt was 0.018780. They agree to 0.08%.
  - My first try was 3.7% off, because I used the salinity of the water
    just below the ice instead of AT the ice base. I had assumed the two
    are almost the same, but the meltwater makes the ice base much
    fresher (by 1.8 g/kg in the first month). I worked out the ice-base
    salinity from the model's own salt balance and the check closed.

10.2 The summary (data/summary_table.csv), all compared with the
  -0.9 C case:
    ocean 1 C warmer (+0.1 C)          2.00 x the melt
    ocean 0.5 C colder (-1.4 C)        0.50 x
    gamma_T doubled                    1.13 x
    gamma_T halved                     0.87 x
    speed-dependent coefficients       0.28 x
    grid twice as fine (year 2)        1.09 x
  The ocean temperature is the biggest control; the FORM of the transfer
  coefficient comes second; its exact size and the grid matter least.

10.3 Back to project 14. There I assumed melt = 10 x T^2 (m/yr, T in C
  above freezing).
  - The exponent here is about 1.5 (steady cases), not 2.
  - The size: melt / TF^2 is only 0.07-0.10 in the isomip box (a long,
    shallow, flat ice shelf where little melts) and 2.7 for the deep ice
    in ISOMIP+. So the right "m" depends on the geometry by a factor of
    30-40, and my value of 10 was too big for both.


========================================================================
11. DOES A FINER GRID CHANGE THE ANSWER? (STEP 6)
========================================================================

The question: is my grid fine enough? If I make the cells smaller and
the answer changes a lot, the coarse answer cannot be trusted.

What I did: repeated the -0.9 C case on a grid with half the spacing in
both directions (0.15 x 0.05 degrees, 100 x 200 points, 4 times as many
columns). Everything else the same.
  - The grid size is fixed when the program is compiled, so this needs
    its own build: experiments/fine_code/SIZE.h, build_fine_fast.
  - experiments/fine_p10/input/data:
        delX = 100*0.15,   delY = 200*0.05,   (the new cell sizes)
        bathyFile = 'bathy_fine.bin',
        nTimeSteps = 35040,   (2 years; the fine grid is 4 times slower)
  - Is the same 30-minute time step still safe? The horizontal friction
    term is the one that can go unstable on small cells. Its "stability
    number" Ah x dt x (1/dx^2 + 1/dy^2) is 0.163 on the smallest cells,
    below the limit of 0.25, so yes.
  - The input files (notebook 06, Part A): the ice shape follows a simple
    rule (it rises 125 m per degree from -700 m at 80 S and stops at
    -200 m), so I can draw it on any grid. I checked the rule against the
    original file first. My first version had a bug (np.maximum instead of
    np.minimum) and the check caught it immediately with a 744 m error.
    After the fix the rule matched to 0.000000000002 m.
  - Run (2 h 12 min):
        bash scripts/run_fine_case.sh ~/mitgcm_runs/isomip/inputs_fine fine_p10

What I found (mean melt, m of ice per year):
            coarse   fine    fine/coarse
  year 1    0.347    0.368   1.06
  year 2    0.239    0.259   1.09
  - The cavity interior hardly changes (deep ice: 1.6% more).
  - The strip right behind the ice front melts 20% more on the fine grid
    (5.55 against 4.64 m/yr), because the narrow band where warm water
    first touches the ice is better resolved.
  So: the interior is close to "converged" (no longer changing with
  resolution), but the ice front is not. My step 3 melt rates would
  probably be about 10% higher on a finer grid.


========================================================================
12. A STANDARD TEST CASE: ISOMIP+ OCEAN0 (STEP 7)
========================================================================

Why: ISOMIP+ is an international comparison where many ice-shelf models
run exactly the same setup. Setting it up myself tests whether I can
follow a published "recipe" and get the expected answer.

The recipe (Asay-Davis et al. 2016, "Ocean0"):
  - a realistic ice-shelf shape from an ice-sheet model (the file
    downloaded in 2.4), in a channel 320-800 km long and 80 km wide,
  - water that is WARMER AND SALTIER with depth (-1.9 C, 33.8 g/kg at the
    top; +1.0 C, 34.7 g/kg at 720 m), so the warm water is heavy,
  - restoring to that profile at the far end (790-800 km),
  - a melt formula where gamma = Gamma_T x (friction velocity), with a
    fixed extra "tidal" speed of 1 cm/s,
  - and the rule: tune Gamma_T until the mean melt where the ice base is
    deeper than 300 m is 30 +/- 2 m per year of water.

What I set up (everything new, written from the paper):
  - A 4 km grid (121 x 21 points, 36 layers of 20 m). The recipe asks
    for 2 km; 4 km keeps each run near an hour on my laptop.
  - Compile time (experiments/isomip_plus_code/): grid size, the rbcs
    package, and a COPY of MITgcm's melt code (shelfice_thermodynamics.F)
    changed to the ISOMIP+ formula. MITgcm's own speed-dependent formula
    is a different one (Holland and Jenkins 1999). Every changed line has
    a comment. The original file in ~/models/MITgcm is untouched.
  - Run time (experiments/isoplus_common/data): a simple straight-line
    equation of state, the Earth's rotation at 75 S, the mixing numbers
    from the paper's Table 4, and the warm profile as the starting state
    and restoring target.
  - The input files (notebook 07, Part A): average the 1 km geometry
    onto 4 km cells; land where more than half a cell is grounded; ice
    thinner than 100 m removed ("calved", 81 cells); cells with less than
    40 m of water under the ice made land (50 cells). Result: 665 ice
    cells, 157 of them deeper than 300 m.

Problems on the way:
  - The paper's formula for the seafloor does not match the official
    file everywhere (up to about 100 m off in places). I used the file
    and wrote this down.
  - "Cannot mix z, p and r": I had written delR where this setup needs
    delZ (layer thicknesses in metres). Changed one word.
  - The first test blew up within 10 days. Very thin cells under the ice
    let water move further than a whole cell in one time step (vertical
    CFL number 1.38). Fix: no cell less than 20% open (hFacMin = 0.2
    instead of 0.1) and a 5-minute time step instead of 10 minutes.
  - My own comment containing the word "NaN" was copied into the model's
    log and would have tripped my NaN alarm. Fixed the comment and taught
    the check to skip copied input lines.

Calibration: four 1-year runs, one per core (about 65 min each). The
  0.1 run went first, on its own, while other runs were still using the
  other cores; then the other three together:
      bash scripts/run_isoplus_cases.sh isoplus_g100
      bash scripts/run_isoplus_cases.sh isoplus_g025 isoplus_g050 isoplus_g200
  Deep melt (m of water per year): Gamma_T 0.025 -> 15.7, 0.05 -> 31.1,
  0.1 -> 51.0, 0.2 -> 67.5. The curve bends: at small Gamma_T the melt is
  almost proportional to it, at large Gamma_T the ocean cannot supply
  heat fast enough (the same lesson as step 4). Interpolating between
  0.025 and 0.05 gives Gamma_T = 0.0483.

The final run: experiments/isoplus_final/ (Gamma_T = 0.0483, 2 years,
1 h 32 min). Deep melt over the last 6 months: 29.53 m/yr, inside the
target band of 28-32, and unchanged from the 6 months before. Whole
shelf: 10.79 m/yr. Strongest melting (68 m/yr) at the deep grounding
line: here the heavy warm water DOES reach the deepest ice, unlike in my
step 3 runs.

Comparison: the paper suggests starting with Gamma_T = 0.022, and the
POP2x model needed about 0.11. I needed 0.048: different models need
different Gamma_T for the same melt, which is exactly why ISOMIP+ asks
everyone to calibrate. Notebook 07 lists all 13 ways my setup differs
from the recipe.

========================================================================
13. THE BIG PICTURE
========================================================================

What the whole project says, in a few lines:
  1. Even with no forcing at all, an ice shelf drives its own
     circulation: the ice pump (deep melting, rising meltwater,
     refreezing higher up), because pressure lowers the freezing point.
  2. A warmer ocean melts more ice, faster than in simple proportion
     (exponent about 1.5 here), because meltwater pulls in more warm
     water. My project 14 assumption of an exponent of 2 had the right
     idea but was a bit too steep for this model.
  3. WHERE the warm water goes matters as much as how warm it is. Warm
     but light water stays near the ice front; warm and salty (heavy)
     water reaches the deep grounding line (ISOMIP+). In the real
     Antarctic, it is the deep grounding-line melting that matters most
     for the ice sheet.
  4. The melt formula's coefficients are uncertain. Their exact size
     matters less than I expected (the ocean limits the heat supply),
     but their FORM (constant or speed-dependent) matters a lot, and so
     does how the model treats very slow currents.
  5. Standard test cases like ISOMIP+ handle this uncertainty by tuning
     the coefficient to a target melt. Different models need different
     values (0.048 for mine, about 0.11 for POP2x).

What it does NOT say: anything about real ice shelves directly. The
geometry is idealized, the ice never changes shape, and there are no
tides or sea ice. The next steps would be to let the ice respond
(coupling to an ice-sheet model) and to look for tipping points in the
coupled ice-ocean system, as in project 14.

15. MISTAKES I MADE AND HOW I CAUGHT THEM
========================================================================

  1. Comparing rounding noise. The first comparison with the reference
     looked poor, because the first steps are pure noise in a perfectly
     still, uniform ocean. Caught by looking at the actual numbers
     (8e-14 in a field that should be uniform). Fix: compare monitor
     times 10-20.
  2. One output file per tile. The first baseline test wrote many small
     files per field. Fix: globalFiles = .TRUE.
  3. Restoring under the ice (step 3, attempt 1). It melted the ice above
     the restoring zone and never reached the deep cavity. Caught by
     splitting the melt into the zone and the rest. Fix: an open-ocean
     zone with no ice above it.
  4. Overwriting a file. I wrote the new restoring mask with the same
     name as the old one, which would have made the first design
     impossible to reproduce. Fix: a new name (rbcs_mask_open.bin), and I
     rebuilt the old file; its checksum matches the original.
  5. Comments in data.shelfice that did not start in the first column
     (" #"). Moved them to column 1, where the model's reader expects
     comment lines.
  6. The fine-grid ice shape: np.maximum instead of np.minimum. Caught
     by checking the rule against the original file (744 m error).
  7. "Cannot mix z, p and r" (ISOMIP+): delR instead of delZ.
  8. ISOMIP+ blew up within 10 days: thin partial cells and a too-long
     time step. Fix: hFacMin 0.2 and a 300 s step.
  9. My comment containing "NaN" would have tripped my own NaN alarm,
     because the model copies the input files into its log. Fix: change
     the comment and skip copied lines in the check.
 10. A misleading circulation number. In step 3 I first reported the
     "largest overturning", which went 136, 118, 77, 163 mSv: not a
     trend at all. Plotting it showed the circulation changes pattern
     (a new cell of the opposite sign), so the largest value was picking
     different cells. Fix: measure the inflow across the ice front.
 11. A reference that was not steady. I started step 4 against the
     -1.9 C case, then saw it was still drifting. Stopped after 8
     minutes and restarted against the -0.9 C case.
 12. The salt check was 3.7% off with the wrong salinity (section 10).
 13. A plotting error: the ISOMIP+ time axis used a 600 s step instead of
     300 s and showed 4 years instead of 2. Caught by comparing the axis
     with the run length.
 14. Wrong escape characters in my notebook-building scripts ("\n"
     turned into a real line break) twice; caught because the notebook
     would not run.
 15. A Python helper assumed the coarse grid size and failed on the fine
     grid. Fix: take the size from the grid files.

16. WORDS TO KNOW (GLOSSARY)
========================================================================

  ice shelf        the floating part of an ice sheet, hundreds of metres
                   thick, with ocean underneath
  cavity           the ocean space under an ice shelf
  grounding line   where the ice lifts off the seafloor and starts to
                   float; the deepest part of the ice base
  ice front        the cliff at the seaward edge of an ice shelf
  ice draft        how deep the ice base is below sea level
  basal melt       melting of the ice shelf from below by the ocean
  freezing point   the temperature at which seawater freezes; lower for
                   saltier water and at higher pressure (depth)
  thermal forcing  how far the water is above its freezing point (TF)
  thermal driving  the same, measured right next to the ice
  ice pump         melting at depth, rising meltwater, refreezing higher
                   up, driven by the pressure effect on freezing
  supercooled      colder than its own freezing point, but still liquid
  marine ice       ice formed by seawater refreezing onto the ice base
  three-equation   the melt formula: heat balance, salt balance and
  model            freezing point at the ice base, solved together
  transfer         gamma_T, gamma_S: how fast heat and salt cross the
  coefficients     thin water layer touching the ice
  friction         a measure of how fast the water rubs along the ice
  velocity (u*)    (m/s); used for speed-dependent coefficients
  Gamma_T          ISOMIP+'s dimensionless number: gamma_T = Gamma_T x u*
  power law        melt = a x TF^n; n is the "exponent"
  restoring        nudging temperature/salinity toward a target in some
  (relaxation)     cells (pkg/rbcs)
  steady           no longer changing (here: less than 2% per year)
  pickup file      a saved model state to restart from
  nIter0           the step number a run starts from (0 = fresh start)
  partial cell     a grid cell that is only partly water (hFacC < 1)
  hFacMin          the smallest allowed open fraction of a cell
  CFL number       how far water moves in one time step compared with a
                   cell size; must stay well below 1
  NaN              "not a number": the model has blown up
  virtual salt     adding fresh water by removing salt instead of adding
  flux             volume
  overturning      the circulation in a north-south (or along-cavity)
                   and depth plane; in Sverdrups (1 Sv = 1 million m^3/s;
                   1 mSv = 1000 m^3/s)
  convergence      the answer stops changing when the grid is refined
  ISOMIP+          the second Ice Shelf-Ocean Model Intercomparison
                   Project: a standard set of test cases
  calibration      tuning an uncertain number until the model hits a
                   target
  IEEE build       a careful, slower build for exact comparisons
  rdmds            the MITgcmutils function that reads MITgcm output
