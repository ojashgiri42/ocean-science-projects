========================================================================
0. THE IDEA IN PLAIN WORDS
========================================================================

West Antarctica's ice sheet sits on a bed that is mostly BELOW sea level
(a "marine" ice sheet). Ice flows from the middle (the "ice divide")
toward the ocean. Where it gets thin enough to float, it lifts off the
bed: that line is the "grounding line". Beyond it, the floating ice is an
"ice shelf".

In many places the bed gets DEEPER going inland (a "reverse slope" or
"overdeepening"). In 1974 Weertman argued that a grounding line there
cannot sit still: if it retreats a little, the ice at the new grounding
line is thicker (the water is deeper), thicker ice flows out faster, so
the ice sheet shrinks more, so the grounding line retreats more... a
runaway. This is the "marine ice sheet instability". It is a tipping
point: once it starts, it keeps going on its own, and putting the
conditions back does not undo it straight away (hysteresis).

Schoof (2007) turned this into a formula: the ice flow across the
grounding line grows with the ice thickness there, to the power 4.75.

What I did in this project:
  1. Used Schoof's formula to find, on paper, where the grounding line
     can be steady, and which positions are unstable.
  2. Wrote my own 1D computer model of an ice sheet (one line from the
     divide to the ocean, a "flowline").
  3. Checked whether my model finds the same positions, and how much its
     answer depends on the grid size (a known weak spot of such models).
  4. Reproduced the hysteresis loop with the real model.
  5. Checked that the model conserves ice, and showed that in 1D an ice
     shelf cannot hold the grounding line back.

Spoiler: the instability and the hysteresis are really there, but a
simple grid can put the grounding line 30-100 km in the wrong place, and
where it ends up even depends on the history of the run.

========================================================================
4. UNITS: THE BORING PART THAT BREAKS EVERYTHING IF IT IS WRONG
========================================================================

Ice moves metres per YEAR, but the papers give A and C per SECOND. I
chose to work in metres and years everywhere, and convert once:

  A (per year) = A (per second) x 31 556 926
      e.g. 1e-25 Pa^-3 s^-1  ->  3.1557e-18 Pa^-3 yr^-1
  C (per year) = C (per second) / 31 556 926^(1/3)
      7.624e6  ->  2.4126e4 Pa m^(-1/3) yr^(1/3)
  (The friction is C times speed^(1/3); speed in m/yr is 31 556 926
  times bigger than in m/s, so C must shrink by that number to the 1/3.)

Two checks that the conversion is right:
  - Schoof's formula computed in seconds and in years gives the same
    flux (relative difference 1.1e-16, i.e. identical).
  - Schoof writes that, with this C, a basal stress of 80 kPa gives
    sliding of "about 35 m/yr". My conversion gives 36.46 m/yr.

5. STEP 1: STEADY STATES ON PAPER, WITH SCHOOF'S FORMULA
========================================================================
(notebooks/01_schoof_steady_states.ipynb)

The question: for a given ice softness A, where can the grounding line
sit still, and is that position stable?

5.1 The bed. It is above sea level near the divide, crosses sea level at
    478.7 km, and between 973.7 km (748.9 m below sea level) and 1265.7 km
    (629.7 m below) it gets SHALLOWER toward the ocean, by 119.2 m. That
    stretch is the overdeepening.

5.2 Flotation thickness: ice floats when it weighs as much as the water
    it pushes aside: thickness H_f = (1000/900) x water depth. At the
    grounding line the ice is exactly this thick.

5.3 Schoof's flux. The ice flow across the grounding line (per metre of
    width):
        q = K x h^4.75,  K = [A (rho_i g)^4 (0.1)^3 / (4^3 C)]^(3/4)
    In plain words: the floating shelf is pulled apart by the difference
    between ice pressure and water pressure, which grows with h; just
    upstream, in a short "boundary layer", the ice slows from shelf-like
    stretching to sliding on the bed. Matching the two gives a flux that
    rises steeply with h. Softer ice (bigger A) flows faster; more
    friction (bigger C) holds it back.

5.4 Steady state = all the snow that falls upstream leaves across the
    grounding line:   a x x_g = q(x_g)
    Plot both against x_g; every crossing is a steady state. (The same
    graphical trick as Stommel's box model in my project 14.)
    Stable or not: if the grounding line moves forward a little, the
    snowfall grows by a per metre and the outflow by dq/dx_g per metre.
    If the outflow grows faster (dq/dx_g > a), the ice sheet loses ice
    and the grounding line moves back: stable. Otherwise: unstable.

    For A = 1e-25 there are three steady states:
        799.8 km   stable
       1124.3 km   unstable (on the reverse slope)
       1376.3 km   stable

5.5 Sweeping A. A trick: the flux is proportional to A^(3/4), so for
    each position x_g I can solve directly for the one A that makes it
    steady. That traces every steady state at once: an S-shaped curve.
    Where it turns around are the "folds":
        A = 4.9296e-26 at x_g = 948.4 km  -> must jump FORWARD 464.6 km
                                             to 1413.0 km
        A = 2.1449e-25 at x_g = 1275.0 km -> must jump BACK 533.7 km
                                             to 741.3 km
    Between these two A values there are two stable positions:
    hysteresis. Four of MISMIP's A values fall inside this range.
    (The unstable stretch, 948-1275 km, is a bit wider than the
    overdeepening, 974-1266 km: just outside it the bed deepens seaward,
    but too gently for the outflow to beat the extra snowfall.)

5.6 The calving front: the farthest steady grounding line in the MISMIP
    range is 1440.7 km (A = 2.5e-26). I put the fixed calving front at
    1600 km, 159 km beyond it. In 1D the length of the shelf does not
    affect the grounding line (section 9), so I only need some room.

Run time: seconds.


========================================================================
6. STEP 2: HOW FAST DOES THE ICE MOVE? (THE VELOCITY SOLVER)
========================================================================
(flowline.py: make_grid, effective_viscosity, basal_drag_coefficient,
 calving_front_stress, solve_ssa; notebooks/02_velocity_solver_and_shelf_test.ipynb)

6.1 The physics. In a fast-sliding ice sheet and in a floating shelf, the
    ice moves like a plug (same speed at every depth): the "shallow-shelf
    approximation" (SSA). At every point three forces balance:
      - stretching: neighbouring ice pulls when the ice is stretched
        (4 x viscosity x thickness x stretching rate, changing along x)
      - friction with the bed: C x speed^(1/3), only under grounded ice
      - the push of gravity down the surface slope ("driving stress")
    At the divide the speed is 0. At the calving front, the stretching
    must balance the ice cliff pushing outward minus the sea pushing back.
    Glen's flow law: ice gets softer the faster it deforms, so the
    viscosity depends on the answer itself (nonlinear).

6.2 The grid is "staggered": thickness in the middle of each cell,
    velocity on the cell edges. Why: the flow of ice from one cell to the
    next happens across an edge; the surface slope between two cell
    middles is an edge value; the stretching between two edges is a
    middle value. Each quantity sits exactly where it is used.

6.3 Picard iteration (how to solve a nonlinear equation): guess a
    velocity, compute the viscosity and friction from it, then the
    equations become linear. Each edge's equation involves only itself
    and its two neighbours, so the system is "tridiagonal", which
    scipy.linalg.solve_banded solves very fast. Repeat with the new
    velocity until it stops changing.
      Key settings in solve_ssa:
        strain_rate_reg=1e-6   floor on the stretching rate (1/yr)
        speed_reg=1e-3         floor on the speed (m/yr)
        tol=1e-10              stop when the change is this small
      Why the floors ("regularization"): at the divide the ice neither
      moves nor stretches, and the viscosity formula would divide by
      zero. A tiny floor keeps it finite and changes nothing where the
      ice really moves.

6.4 The test. A floating shelf of uniform thickness has an exact
    answer: it stretches at a constant rate
        du/dx = A (rho_i g (1 - rho_i/rho_w) H / 4)^3
    For H = 500 m and A = 1e-25: 4.2289e-3 per year, 845.786 m/yr at
    the front of a 200 km shelf.
    Result: error 5.592e-8 at EVERY grid spacing (20 km to 0.625 km).
    The exact answer is a straight line, which my scheme reproduces
    exactly, so the only error left is the floor from 6.3. Proof: floors
    of 1e-4, 1e-5, 1e-6, 1e-7 gave errors 5.585e-4, 5.592e-6, 5.592e-8,
    5.590e-10, exactly (floor / stretching rate)^2.

6.5 A better test. Because the straight line hides any grid error, I
    added a shelf that thins from 1000 m to 400 m. The same formula then
    holds at every point with the local thickness (I showed why), so the
    exact velocity is a curve. The error fell from 1.552e-3 (20 km) to
    1.506e-6 (0.625 km): divided by 4 each time the grid spacing is
    halved, a "convergence rate" of 2.001. That is what this kind of
    scheme should give.

6.6 Speed: 0.003 s per solve with 10 cells, 0.021 s with 320 cells.
    Picard from zero needs about 77 iterations (each one cuts the change
    by a factor 0.667); in time stepping it starts from the last
    velocity and needs far fewer.

(Cuffey and Paterson confirm the test formula is the right one for a 1D
flowline: a shelf that cannot spread sideways spreads 9/8 times faster
than one that can, their "about 10% faster".)


========================================================================
7. STEP 3: LETTING THE ICE SHEET EVOLVE, AND THE GRID PROBLEM
========================================================================
(flowline.py: grounded_fraction_edges, grounding_line_position,
 edge_flux, stable_time_step, run_to_steady_state;
 notebooks/03_time_dependent_model_and_resolution.ipynb)

7.1 Mass conservation: each cell's thickness changes by snowfall plus
    ice flowing in minus ice flowing out:
        H_new = H + dt x (a - (flux out - flux in) / dx)
    The flux uses the thickness of the cell the ice comes FROM
    ("upwind"); averaging both sides lets wiggles grow.

7.2 The time step: an explicit update is only stable if no ice moves
    more than one cell per step. I use half a cell:
        dt = 0.5 x dx / (fastest speed), never more than 10 years
    The fastest ice (about 2000 m/yr at the front) sets the step, so
    halving the grid spacing costs about 4 times more (twice the cells,
    half the step).

7.3 Grounded or floating: a cell is grounded if its ice is thicker than
    the flotation thickness. Surface = bed + H if grounded,
    0.1 x H above sea level if floating.

7.4 Starting point, as in MISMIP: a 10 m layer of ice everywhere. It
    floats where the bed is more than 9 m below sea level, so the first
    grounding line is at 481.6 km.

7.5 When is a run "finished"? I first used a loose rule and got steady
    states 30-100 km short of Schoof. Before believing that, I ran the
    same cases for a fixed 60 000 years: the grounding lines stopped
    moving after about 20 000 years and the volume changed by 0.000% in
    the last 10 000. So they really are stuck. Then I tightened the rule:
        XG_RATE_TOL = 0.1    grounding line moves less than 0.1 m/yr
        DHDT_TOL    = 1e-4   thickness changes less than 0.1 mm/yr
        CHECK_EVERY = 500    checked every 500 years

7.6 The resolution test: A = 3e-25 (Schoof: 721.90 km, on a normal bed),
    from the 10 m layer to steady state, at 8, 4, 2, 1 and 0.5 km.
    Each took 18 500 to 22 500 model years.

    plain fixed grid:
        8 km  615.44 km (106.45 km short)     3 s
        4 km  639.79 km  (82.10 short)        7 s
        2 km  659.94 km  (61.96 short)       17 s
        1 km  676.99 km  (44.91 short)       45 s
      0.5 km  691.50 km  (30.40 short)      137 s

    Why a fixed grid struggles:
      - friction switches from full to zero at one grid edge, while in
        reality it changes over a short boundary layer;
      - the grounding line can only move a whole cell at a time: to
        advance, the next cell must thicken until it grounds. So the grid
        can hold the grounding line in the wrong place.

7.7 The sub-grid scheme (after Gladstone et al. 2010, Leguy et al.
    2014). "Thickness above flotation" is positive on grounded ice and
    negative on floating ice. Between the last grounded and the first
    floating cell I draw a straight line and place the grounding line
    where it crosses zero, then give that one edge only the grounded
    fraction of its friction. Example: +25 m and -15 m, 8 km apart ->
    the grounding line is 25/40 = 0.625 of the way across, and the edge
    gets 0.625 of its friction. One line in the code switches it:
        run_to_steady_state(..., "subgrid", ...)   instead of "plain"

    sub-grid scheme:
        8 km  691.84 km (30.06 short)     3.5 s
        4 km  705.78 km (16.11 short)     8 s
        2 km  710.98 km (10.92 short)    17 s
        1 km  715.48 km  (6.42 short)    44 s
      0.5 km  717.73 km  (4.16 short)   112 s
    At 8 km it is as good as the plain grid at 0.5 km, but it is still
    not exactly right even at 0.5 km.

7.8 Advance or retreat? On coarse grids I also ran to steady state with
    A = 1e-25 first, then switched to 3e-25 so the grounding line had to
    RETREAT into place:
      plain grid: it barely retreated (1 to 3 km) and stopped after
        1500 years, 45-59 km away from where the advance stopped.
      sub-grid: advance and retreat end on either side of Schoof's
        721.90 km (at 2 km: 710.98 and 725.70 km); the gap shrinks with
        resolution (67, 30, 15 km at 8, 4, 2 km).
    So on a coarse grid the answer depends on the history of the run,
    not only on the physics. Important for step 4.

8. STEP 4: THE HYSTERESIS LOOP IN THE REAL MODEL
========================================================================
(notebooks/04_hysteresis_experiment.ipynb)

Settings at the top of the notebook:
    A_SEQUENCE_SI = 3e-25 ... 2.5e-26 ... 3e-25   (MISMIP Table 5)
    DX = 1e3          1 km grid
    SCHEME = "subgrid"
    CHECK_EVERY = 100  (years; also how often a profile is saved)
    MAX_YEARS_PER_STEP = 80000

Each A starts from the final ice sheet of the previous A and runs until
steady. All 13 steps reached steady state: 221 500 model years in 5.5
minutes (much faster than my 20-40 minute guess).

What happened:
  Going down (A smaller = stiffer ice = bigger ice sheet):
    715.5, 725.5, 739.4, 758.5, 792.5, 908.5 km ... then at A = 2.5e-26
    a JUMP to 1433.5 km.
  Going up:
    1407.1, 1372.1, 1343.0, 1307.9 km ... then at A = 2.5e-25 a JUMP
    back to 730.9 km, and 720.9 km at the end.
  So for A = 5e-26, 1e-25, 1.5e-25 and 2e-25 the model has two
  different steady states depending on where it came from. The jumps
  happen between the same MISMIP values as Schoof's folds.
  Errors against Schoof: mostly 3 to 7 km, except 17.6 km at A = 5e-26,
  right next to the lower fold, where the ice sheet also took 31 600
  years to settle. Near a fold the steady-state curve is almost
  vertical, so small errors matter a lot, and the system responds very
  slowly ("critical slowing down", the same effect behind the early
  warning signals in project 14).

The jumps take a long time:
  advance: crossed the overdeepening in 5800 years (about 50 m/yr)
  retreat: 3800 years just to reach the overdeepening, then 7500 years
    to cross it, speeding up to 167 m/yr near its landward end: the
    runaway feeds itself.


========================================================================
9. STEP 5: CHECKING THE BOOKKEEPING, AND WHAT A SHELF CAN'T DO
========================================================================
(notebooks/05_checks_and_summary.ipynb)

9.1 Mass check. Added up over all cells, the ice moving between cells
    cancels out, so the total ice volume can only change by snowfall
    minus the ice leaving at the calving front. My run (sub-grid, 4 km):
    volume grew by 1.826951e9 m^2, snowfall minus calving was
    1.826951e9 m^2. Relative difference 1.9e-14: only rounding. In
    steady state, snowfall and calving rates differ by 0.033%.

9.2 In 1D the shelf can't hold the grounding line back.
    In step 2 I showed that the stress in a free shelf at any point
    depends only on the thickness there. At the grounding line that
    thickness is fixed by the water depth, so nothing downstream
    matters.
      - Halving the shelf thickness: the grounded ice speed changed by
        at most 7.2e-10 m/yr. (Only the shelf slowed, 2038 to 591 m/yr.)
      - Melting the shelf from below at 2 and 10 m/yr (new option
        shelf_melt=... in run_to_steady_state): the shelf thinned from
        258 m to 29.6 and 8.5 m, and the grounding line moved -0.11 and
        0.00 km.

9.3 A trap I fell into: my first version melted EVERY floating cell,
    including the first one after the grounding line. With 10 m/yr the
    grounding line then retreated 36.81 km in 5000 years, jumping back
    and forth. That is the grid, not physics: on a 4 km grid that cell's
    centre can be almost 4 km from the grounding line, so I was melting
    ice that is nearly still grounded. Not melting that cell (the default
    now; melt_first_floating_cell=True brings the old behaviour back)
    removed the retreat completely. Recent papers (Seroussi and
    Morlighem 2018; Wang et al. 2024) recommend exactly this.

9.4 Why this matters for real Antarctica (and my project 16): real
    shelves sit in bays and rest on bumps, so they push back on the
    grounded ice ("buttressing"). Schoof writes this as a factor theta
    on the stress at the grounding line, which multiplies the flux by
    theta^(9/4). Thinning a buttressing shelf by ocean melt raises theta,
    increases the flux, and can push the grounding line onto a reverse
    slope, where the instability takes over. My 1D model cannot do this
    at all, which is exactly why the 3D ocean melt of project 16 matters.


========================================================================
10. THE BIG PICTURE
========================================================================

  1. On a bed that deepens inland, a marine ice sheet has unstable
     grounding-line positions, folds, jumps of hundreds of kilometres,
     and hysteresis. Schoof's formula predicts them on paper; my own
     model reproduces them.
  2. The steady states come from one simple balance (snowfall in = ice
     out), found with the same graphical method as my box models in
     project 14.
  3. Numerical models can get the grounding line wrong by tens of
     kilometres, and the error depends on the grid and on the history.
     A simple sub-grid fix helps a lot but does not remove it. Real
     model studies of Antarctic tipping (like Garbe et al. 2020) say
     their thresholds depend on resolution; now I understand why.
  4. Crossing an overdeepening takes thousands of years, and a retreat
     speeds itself up.
  5. In 1D the ocean cannot touch the grounding line through the shelf;
     in the real world it can, through buttressing.

What the next steps would be: couple this model to a simple ocean box
model so ocean temperature melts the shelf, add Schoof's buttressing
factor so melting matters, and compare updating ocean and ice together
every step (synchronous) with updating the ocean only now and then
(asynchronous), especially near a tipping point.

12. MISTAKES I MADE AND HOW I CAUGHT THEM
========================================================================

  1. A test that could not fail. My uniform-shelf test had the same
     error at every grid spacing. Instead of celebrating, I asked why:
     the exact answer was a straight line, which the scheme reproduces
     exactly. I added a curved test (thinning shelf) to see the real
     convergence.
  2. A loose "steady state" rule. First steady states were 30-100 km
     short of Schoof. I ran 60 000 years to make sure they were truly

========================================================================
13. WORDS TO KNOW (GLOSSARY)
========================================================================

  ice sheet          a large mass of ice on land
  marine ice sheet   an ice sheet whose bed is below sea level
  ice divide         the line from which ice flows away on both sides
  grounding line     where the ice lifts off the bed and starts to float
  ice shelf          the floating part of the ice
  calving front      the end of the ice shelf, where icebergs break off
  overdeepening /    a stretch where the bed gets deeper going inland
  reverse slope
  flotation          the thickness at which ice just floats:
  thickness          (water density / ice density) x water depth
  rate factor A      how soft the ice is (warmer ice = bigger A)
  Glen's flow law    ice deforms faster under more stress, to the
                     power n = 3
  sliding law        friction with the bed = C x speed^m, m = 1/3
  SSA                shallow-shelf approximation: ice moves like a plug
  driving stress     the push of gravity down the surface slope
  viscosity          how strongly ice resists stretching
  Picard iteration   solve a nonlinear problem by repeatedly fixing the
                     coefficients from the last answer
  tridiagonal        each equation involves only a point and its two
                     neighbours (fast to solve)
  regularization     a tiny floor that stops a division by zero
  staggered grid     different quantities at cell centres and edges
  upwind             take the value from the side the flow comes from
  CFL / time step    no ice may move more than (half) a cell per step
  steady state       nothing changes any more
  stable / unstable  a small push dies away / grows
  fold               where a branch of steady states ends and the system
                     must jump
  hysteresis         the state depends on the history, not only on the
                     current conditions
  critical slowing   near a fold the system returns to steady state
  down               very slowly
  convergence rate   how fast the error falls as the grid gets finer
                     (2 means: half the spacing, a quarter of the error)
  sub-grid scheme    placing the grounding line between grid points and
                     scaling the friction in that one cell
  buttressing        an ice shelf held by bays or bumps pushing back on
                     the grounded ice (impossible in 1D)
  MISMIP             Marine Ice Sheet Model Intercomparison Project: a
                     standard test that many models ran
